How Galaxy Harmonix turns video into sound

Sonification is the use of sound to represent data. Here the data is a video: every second, one frame is sampled and turned into one second of audio, so the soundtrack stays in sync with the picture.

1. Scan two rings for stars

Two square rings are placed around the centre of the frame, at 55% and 90% of the distance to the nearest edge. Each ring is cut into 10×10 pixel boxes. A box is "hot" if it contains any pixel brighter than the threshold you choose (0–255, converted to grayscale).

2. Map brightness to pitch

Each hot box becomes a note. The more bright pixels it holds, the higher the note, from 500 Hz up to 1800 Hz. The inner ring plays its notes in the first half of the second and the outer ring in the second half, so a busy star field sounds like a fast arpeggio and an empty one stays quiet. At most 12 of the brightest boxes per ring are used, so notes stay long enough to hear.

Underneath, the average red level of the whole frame drives a low drone between 40 and 160 Hz: warm, red nebulae hum louder and higher than dark, blue space.

3. Give the notes a timbre

A pure sine wave sounds thin, so notes are built from a fundamental plus overtones. The drone borrows its timbre from a real recording: a Fourier transform finds the strongest frequencies in the clip, snaps them to harmonics of the target pitch, and uses their relative strengths as overtone weights.

4. Shape, mix and mux

Every star note is shaped by an ADSR envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release) so it starts and fades like a struck bell instead of clicking on and off. Star notes and drone are summed, normalised to 16-bit audio, and ffmpeg muxes the result onto the original video. Results are cached per video and threshold, so revisiting a combination is instant.

Run it yourself

The engine is a plain Python package and works without the website:

python -m processing my_video.mp4 --threshold 80
